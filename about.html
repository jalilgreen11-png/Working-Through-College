About this project

A working student's guide to managing the semester at Cal State LA.

Why this site exists

I work full-time as an Operations Manager at a healthcare agency in Los Angeles, and I am also a CIS undergraduate at Cal State LA. Like many students here, I balance full-time work, classes, family responsibilities, and other commitments outside of school.

The standard college advice does not always fit that reality. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (October 2023), 39.6 percent of full-time college students and 77.7 percent of part-time college students are employed. At Cal State LA, U.S. News (2024) reports a four-year graduation rate of 25 percent, which suggests that most students are taking longer than four years to finish, often because they are working alongside school.

This site is a starting point. It is not a complete answer. It is a small set of practical systems that working students can try, adjust, and share.

The Four Systems, Explained

1. Build a Realistic Weekly Schedule

The first system is a schedule that reflects how a week actually goes, not how a student wishes it went. That means writing down every fixed block first. Work shifts. Class times. Commute. Sleep. Any commitments outside of school. What is left is the time available for studying.

Tools like Google Calendar work well for this, especially when blocks are color-coded so the week is easy to read at a glance. A schedule that ignores real constraints tends to fall apart by midweek. Planning for the realistic version of a day, not an ideal version, makes a noticeable difference.

2. Manage Energy, Not Just Time

Time management advice often treats every hour as equal. It is not. An hour after a long shift is not the same as an hour on a Saturday morning. Identifying which hours of the week are best for harder thinking, and protecting those hours for harder coursework, often produces better results than simply adding more study time.

Lower-energy windows are better suited to lighter tasks like reviewing notes, organizing materials, or planning the next day. Heavier work like writing or new material belongs in the better windows.

Sleep matters here too. Research on working students suggests that working more than about 15 hours a week while enrolled full-time can affect GPA. For students working 30 or 40 hours, sleep becomes one of the few variables they actually control.

3. Communicate Early With Employers and Professors

Many working students wait too long to have hard conversations. They do not mention finals week to a manager until after the schedule is posted, or do not email a professor until the night an assignment is due. By then, options are limited.

Reaching out early and in writing tends to produce better outcomes. A short, specific message to a manager before midterms, or to a professor before a conflict happens, is usually received well. Most professors and managers are familiar with working students and respond more openly to early communication than to last-minute requests.

4. Adjust Course Load When Needed

Sometimes the issue is not the system but the size of the load. Eighteen units, 40 hours of work, family responsibilities, and other commitments cannot all run at full intensity in the same semester without something giving way.

Adjusting a course load is not failure. Reducing units to ensure passing grades in remaining classes, requesting fewer hours during finals week, or shifting responsibilities temporarily are practical options. The goal is finishing the degree. The path to that goal is flexible.
